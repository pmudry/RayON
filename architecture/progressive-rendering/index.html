    Progressive Rendering¶

    The interactive modes (3 for CUDA, 5 for OptiX) are where RayON feels most alive. This page explains how the accumulation loop keeps the window responsive while the image converges, and how the OptiX renderer holds its frame rate while the camera moves.

    Overview¶

    The interactive host (renderer_optix_progressive_host.hpp, renderer_cuda_progressive_host.hpp) runs one loop:

    1. Poll SDL events: mouse, keyboard, window resize, the Dear ImGui panel.
    2. If the camera or a scene parameter changed, reset the accumulation (zero the float buffer on the device, restart the sample counter).
    3. Launch one batch of samples per pixel on the GPU and add it to the accumulation buffer.
    4. Convert the buffer to the 8-bit display image on the GPU, copy it to pinned host memory, upload it to the SDL texture, draw the panel, present.
    sequenceDiagram
    actor User
    participant SDL
    participant Host as Host (C++)
    participant GPU
    User->>SDL: drag the mouse
    SDL-->>Host: SDL_MOUSEMOTION
    Host->>GPU: reset accumulation (memset on the render stream)
    loop every frame
        Host->>GPU: launch(samples_per_batch, samples_so_far)
        GPU-->>Host: uint8 display buffer (async copy, pinned memory)
        Host->>SDL: SDL_UpdateTexture + SDL_RenderCopy + ImGui
        SDL-->>User: frame
    end

    There is no fixed schedule of sample stages and no pause between batches: as long as the camera is still, every frame adds Samples/Batch samples (8 by default, per-scene override with settings.samples_per_batch), until the accumulation is complete.

    Float accumulation on the GPU¶

    The accumulation buffer is a float4 array of width × height that never leaves the device. Each kernel launch adds its samples to it and keeps the total count; a small gamma kernel then converts the running mean to 8-bit RGB:

    float r = sqrtf(accum[i].x / total_samples);   // gamma 2.0 = sqrt

    Only the 3-byte-per-pixel display image crosses to the host, through an asynchronous copy into page-locked memory on a dedicated stream, so the next batch can start while the previous frame is being displayed (see Optimisations, items 7, 11 and 17).

    The sampler state (Sobol' index, scramble seed) is also persistent per pixel, and the scramble is refreshed on every restart so that consecutive accumulations do not replay the same sequence.

    When does it stop?¶

    Condition Default
    Adaptive sampling reports 99.9 % of the pixels converged on
    The per-pixel budget is reached 100 000 samples
    Accumulation paused with Space or --no-auto-accumulate —

    Adaptive sampling¶

    Each pixel tracks its own sample count and the statistics of its value. After 32 samples, a pixel whose standard error in display space falls below the threshold (0.01 of the 0–1 range, about 2.5 levels of 255, adjustable in the GUI) is flagged as converged and skipped by later batches; the GPU counts the converged pixels with a warp-shuffle reduction so only one integer comes back to the host. Pixels that are lit only by rare bright paths keep sampling. The Show Sample Heatmap option paints the samples spent per pixel.

    Camera motion (OptiX)¶

    A camera move invalidates the accumulation, so during an orbit every frame is a one-batch image. At full resolution that is a few frames per second on a heavy scene. The OptiX host therefore trades quality for frame rate while the camera moves only:

    flowchart LR
    A["camera event"] --> B["moving: render at a fraction of the\nresolution with few samples,\nupscale for display"]
    B -->|"no event for the cooldown (50 ms)"| C["still: full resolution,\nSamples/Batch per frame,\naccumulate"]
    C -->|"camera event"| B

    The regulator holds a target frame rate (60 fps by default). It models the cost of a motion frame from the previous ones and, when the frame rate drops, lowers the render fraction first (from the ceiling of 3/4 down to a floor of 1/4 of the width and height, that is 1/16 of the pixels), then the samples per frame (down to a floor of 4). When there is headroom it raises them back. Every value is a slider in the Camera Motion section of the panel; the readout shows what the last motion frame used.

    On top of the regulator, the motion frames can use one sample per pixel, the cheaper PCG sampler, a capped bounce depth, skip the shadow rays, be despeckled at display time, and be passed through the OptiX AI denoiser (guided by the albedo and normal of the first hit). The denoiser can keep running over the first still frames with a weight that fades to zero at 64 samples, so the image does not snap from smooth to noisy when the camera stops. Optional intermediate stages (1/2 then 3/4 resolution, each fading into the previous image) are available for very heavy scenes.

    The CUDA interactive renderer has none of this: it keeps rendering full batches while the camera moves, with the display-time despeckle as its only concession.

    Camera interaction¶

    Input Action
    Left mouse + drag Orbit around the look-at point
    Right mouse + drag Pan the camera and the look-at point
    Scroll wheel Zoom
    O Auto orbit
    Left / Right Previous / next scene

    The orbit keeps the camera on a sphere around the look-at point in spherical coordinates; the pan moves both points along the camera's right and up vectors. Any change restarts the accumulation; the GUI's Save camera & lights button writes the current view into the scene file.

    Live parameter updates¶

    Most sliders change a value that is passed to the next launch and restart the accumulation. Nothing is rebuilt:

    • OptiX: background intensity, light intensity and falloff, depth of field, the MIS and sampler options are fields of the launch parameters, uploaded (a few hundred bytes) before every launch. The cloud sliders rewrite one material record and re-bake its density field.
    • CUDA: the same values are __constant__ symbols updated with cudaMemcpyToSymbol, and the material record is rewritten in place.

    Changing the scene rebuilds everything: the acceleration structures, the material and light tables, the textures and the volume grids.
